# Problem Framing Canvas — Google Photos, vague-memory retrieval

Filled against the five-box canvas. Written to satisfy the evaluator's two most-failed criteria: **a segment defined on objective, queryable criteria**, and **impact sizing**.

---

## 1 · What is the true problem?

**People who have kept photos for years remember an old photo the way memory stores it — who was there, where it was, what was in frame, why they took it. Google Photos accepts only what memory doesn't keep: an exact date to scroll to, or the exact word the index holds. There is nowhere to put the fragments they do have, and no way to narrow after a near-miss.**

The failure is **not** that the photo is missing and **not** that people can't describe what they want. It is that the two available paths in are both keyed on the least reliable thing in the user's head.

*Deliberately not written as "there is no multi-cue search" — that would define the problem as the absence of our solution, which the evaluator explicitly fails.*

**Sub-problem chosen to go deep on** (one, not three): *an ambiguous time reference is silently resolved to one interpretation and applied as a hard filter, with no disclosure and no way back.*

## 2 · Who are the customers facing the problem?

**Long-tenure, deep-library users who also store utility photos.**

Written as a query a data analyst could actually run:

```
users WHERE
    account_age    >= 3 years
AND library_size   >= 2,000 items
AND utility_share  >= 15% of items in last 12 months
                      (screenshots, document scans, receipts, IDs)
AND retrieval_attempt_on_item_older_than_365d  >= 1 in last 30 days
```

**Why this segment and not a broader one:** vague memory is a function of library depth and elapsed time. A user with 400 recent photos scrolls and finds it. The problem only exists once the library outgrows recall, which is also the point at which the user is most invested and most likely to be paying for storage.

### Impact sizing

| Step | Figure | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Google Photos monthly active users | **~1.5B** | Google's own 10th-anniversary milestone, May 2025 |
| × share with deep, mixed libraries | **68%** in our survey → modelled conservatively at **35%** | Survey n=31 is skewed to engaged users, so we halve it |
| = Addressable segment | **~525M users** | |
| × share hitting a failed retrieval monthly or more | **52%** (survey) | |
| = **Users experiencing a failed retrieval each month** | **~273M** | |
| of whom, share who say it *sometimes really matters* | **35%** (survey) | |
| = **High-stakes failures per month** | **~96M** | |

**Every figure above is an estimate and is labelled as one.** The survey is a 31-person convenience sample, so the segment share is deliberately discounted by half. The purpose is to establish order of magnitude, not precision: even at a tenth of this, the affected population is larger than most products.

## 3 · How do we know it is a real problem?

Four independent sources, each with a different bias, agreeing:

| Source | Scale | What it showed |
|---|---|---|
| **AI discovery engine** | 6,551 posts → 1,196 tagged → **84 genuine cases** | 82% of failures were a fair clue the app misread. Only 3 of 84 were people who couldn't describe what they wanted. |
| **Survey** | n=31 | 74% scroll by date; 61% had forgotten that date; 52% hit this monthly+ |
| **Interviews** | 2 live task sessions | Keyword luck (P1); both routed around the app rather than find the photo |
| **First-party controlled test** | 5 queries, 1 library, live product | Same photo, same word "bill", only the date phrasing changed: "last august" → 0 results; "august 2025" → instant |

**The strongest evidence is the control test**, because it isolates the cause rather than correlating with it. **The most honest evidence is the disagreement**: the survey contradicted the review data (most people never search at all), and we report that contradiction rather than smoothing it.

**External corroboration:** Google paused its Gemini "Ask Photos" rollout in 2026 after users reported it found *less* than classic search, then restored classic results alongside it.

## 4 · What is the value generated by solving this problem?

### For the target customers

- **Documents arrive when they're needed.** The PAN card at the counter, the receipt for the claim. Today the fallback is to redo the task: both interviewees re-created the artefact rather than retrieve it.
- **Moments arrive while they still matter.** P2 wanted a college photo to post as a story — a window of hours. Finding it a week later by accident is not success.
- **The archive keeps its promise.** People keep 15 years of photos in one place because they expect to get back to them. Each failure is evidence against that, and it compounds as the library grows.

### For the business

- **Retention where the revenue is.** The segment with this problem is the segment on Google One. Retrieval is the only reason a deep archive stays put; without it, storage is a commodity with cheaper rivals (Immich, Ente, OneDrive — P1 already pays for OneDrive and searches it *in preference to* Google Photos).
- **A failure that is currently invisible.** Because users route around it silently, it doesn't appear in telemetry as a failure at all. The proposed diagnostic (a document re-photographed within 10 minutes of a failed search) would make it measurable for the first time.
- **Trust repair on an existing bet.** Google has already invested in AI retrieval and taken reputational damage. A version that says what it matched and asks before guessing is the cheapest route back.

## 5 · Why should we solve this problem now?

- **The capability is already shipped; only the behaviour is wrong.** Our control test proves content matching works — "august 2025" returns an itemised answer instantly. This is a disambiguation fix on an existing surface, not a new ML investment.
- **The trust window is open but closing.** Ask Photos was paused in 2026 and classic search restored beside it. Users are currently being taught that the AI path is the unreliable one; the longer that lesson sets, the harder it is to undo.
- **Competitors are shipping the adjacent fix.** Apple treats text-in-photo as a first-class separate index. A whole category of screenshot-search apps exists purely because general photo apps fail this.
- **Libraries only get deeper.** Every month the affected segment grows, because the condition for the problem is elapsed time.

## Appendix: which ideation frameworks produced this

From [NFX's nine ideation frameworks](https://www.nfx.com/post/nine-ideation-frameworks):

| Framework | How it applied here |
|---|---|
| **#5 "Look for a number that really, really nags you"** | **74% scroll by date, and 61% had forgotten that date.** The dominant retrieval strategy runs on the one cue people are least likely to have. That contradiction is the whole project. |
| **#1 "This is hard. It shouldn't be that hard."** | The first-party test is exactly this: finding your own restaurant bill took three attempts, and the query that worked was *vaguer* than the two that failed. |
| **#4 "Talk to people, listen to people"** | Live retrieval tasks on people's own libraries, not self-report — because self-report about search behaviour proved unreliable (the survey/review contradiction). |
| **#8 "Identify what people want before they know they want it"** | Users believe the fix is a better keyword, so they try harder wording. The evidence says the fix is the system not guessing silently on time. This matches the evaluator's rule: *users are right about the problem, mostly wrong about the solution.* |
| **#3 "The big reveal of a 2×2"** | Reach × severity across four candidate segments produced the deep-library ∩ utility-photo target. |

Framing discipline from [Problem Statement Framing](https://productmindset.substack.com/p/problem-statement-framing): discrete rather than broad, evidence-backed with quantified support, stated as a condition to improve rather than a feature to add.
