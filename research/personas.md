# Personas — vague-memory photo retrieval

**These are syntheses, not people.** Each one is assembled from five task-based
interviews (P1–P5), a 31-person survey, and 84 genuine vague-memory failures
pulled out of 1,196 tagged public posts. Every claim below carries its source.
Nothing here is invented, and no persona is a stand-in for a single
participant — where a line comes from one session, it says so.

Biographical detail is left out on purpose. Age, job and city did not predict
anything in this research; **library depth and how old the photo is** predicted
everything. A persona built on demographics would be decoration.

---

## 1. The Deep Archive

**Shape:** 4+ years, tens of thousands of items, mixed camera photos,
screenshots, WhatsApp downloads. One primary account.
*Evidence: P2 (~75,000 photos in 4 years); 20 of 31 survey respondents have 5+
years of library.*

**How they retrieve:** scrolls first, searches only when scrolling fails — and
often not even then. P2 found a document by recalling roughly when they took
it, scrolling to that week, failing, **using surrounding photos as landmarks to
correct their own date estimate by about a month**, then scrolling again. Five
steps, two passes, one document. The app was never in the loop.

**Where it breaks:** the one cue the scroll depends on is the one they are least
sure of. 61% of survey respondents named the exact date as the thing they had
forgotten — the single most forgotten cue — while 74% scroll by date.

**The feature they trust most fails them specifically.** P2 tried face search for
a 20-year-old photo: *"eventually not finding there because the image is of 20
years ago which doesn't resemble with current faces."* P3, with 5,000 photos,
succeeded at the same task with the same feature. **Face search degrades with
depth and elapsed time**, which is the segment definition.

**What they asked for, unprompted:** *"A feature to filter pictures based on
non-recorded criteria like (a picture with child / black and white picture)."*

**What success looks like:** P2 wanted a college photo to post as a story. That
window is hours. Finding it a week later while scrolling is not success.

## 2. The Paperwork Keeper

**Shape:** photographs bills, IDs, receipts and screens instead of filing them.
Not a separate kind of person — an overlapping behaviour. 77% of survey
respondents do this.

**This is the loudest finding in the research.** Three of four interviewees named
a **document** as their last real retrieval hassle: a PAN card, a recovery
password screenshot, an electricity bill. P4 failed the document task outright:
*"Given it was a document, I was unable to exactly locate it."*

**Where it breaks:** the right word is not the obvious word. P1 searched for a
receipt by brand (**"Bosch"**) and got photos of the appliance, not the receipt;
by vendor name, nothing; by the generic word **"bill"**, found it. His summary:
*"The keyword matters."* Remembering the content correctly was not enough — he
had to guess the term the index had chosen.

**The workaround is invisible.** They do not file a complaint, they leave:
- P3: *"I personally place receipts and docs on g drive directly"* — the use case
  is lost at capture, before any search happens.
- P1 keeps his real archive in a paid OneDrive account and searches there by
  preference.
- P1 gave up after ~2 minutes and **re-photographed the document**.

**None of this registers as a failed search.** It is a product losing a job to
another app, silently.

## 3. The Shallow Library

**Shape:** 3,000–5,000 photos over 6–8 years. Mostly screenshots and videos.
*Evidence: P3, P4.*

**How they retrieve:** scrolls, and usually it works, because previews load fast
and the library is small enough to skim. P4 expected to struggle *"but only for
a few seconds."*

**Where it breaks:** rarely — and that is the point. P3 calls it *"a slight
inconvenience."* When it does break, they give up fastest, because they have the
least practice at recovering.

**Why they are in the research but not the target.** P3 succeeded at the
date-blind task that P2 failed, using the same feature. Shallow libraries are
not where this problem lives. They are the control that proves the segment is
about **depth and age, not people**.

**One failure mode only they surfaced.** P4, on a date-blind photo: *"I don't
even know if it exists on this device or not. I know it exists. But if it
actually exists on this device, I am unable to locate it."* They cannot tell
*missing* from *unfindable*. The archive's core promise fails a level below
retrieval.

## What the personas share, and what they do not

| | Deep Archive | Paperwork Keeper | Shallow Library |
|---|---|---|---|
| First move | scroll | scroll, even for text | scroll |
| Frequency | weekly–monthly | weekly for documents | rarely |
| Does it matter? | yes, and time-bound | yes, needed now | mild annoyance |
| Workaround | ask someone, wait | store elsewhere, redo it | give up |
| In the target segment? | **yes** | **yes** | no |

**The common thread is not "they cannot describe the photo".** All four
described their photos fluently. P4 wanted to say *"It's that day!"* — an
occasion, not a calendar value. P3 wanted to *"describe the contents and
specific details in the picture."* The gap is between what they can say and what
the product can take.

## Two cautions a reviewer should know

**These rest on five interviews, not twelve.** Four of the five named a document
as their last real hassle; one of them (P1) was not primarily a Google Photos
user, and his live retrieval happened in OneDrive. That session is evidence
about photo retrieval generally, not about Google Photos specifically.

**One hypothesis died and then came back.** Multi-account fragmentation looked
promising in the survey, failed three interviews in a row — P1, P2 and P3 all
have several accounts and none reported a retrieval problem — and was dropped.
P5 then said plainly that not knowing which account a photo is in has made it
harder to find. One data point either way, so it is back as an open question
rather than a finding. The deck says exactly that.

---

## A late addition worth knowing

P5 reported that Google Photos now groups documents into their own category, and
reaches for it. That is a real mitigation shipping inside the product, and it
narrows the paperwork gap this research argues from. It does not close it — P5's
own last hassle was still a document, a month earlier — because the category
helps you **browse** documents and still does nothing to help you say **which**
document. Expect a reviewer to raise it.
